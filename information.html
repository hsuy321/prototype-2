Thirty Stock Images: Map, Index, Information

Thirty Stock Images is an exhibition of thirty images placed in newsracks around Manhattan.

The thirty images in the exhibition derive from the same stock image, Young woman facing ocean sunset rejoices, laughs, smiles looking up to the sky, enjoys life and summer, nature, happiness. by kieferpix on Adobe Stock. The stock image was downloaded from Adobe Stock using my Adobe Education License.

On the back of each image is a corresponding title, taken from thirty webpages that use this stock image. The titles range from inspirational (The Secrets of Happiness) to nondescript (All Contract jobs near Germany). Each title creates a different approach to viewing the stock image.

Stock photography was popularized in the 1920s, when newspapers, magazines, and advertisers began purchasing ready-made images as a more affordable and efficient alternative to commissioned photography.1 Today, stock photography is built into the visual language of everyday life, from physical posters to digital advertisements.

1 “Stock Photography,” Wikipedia.

When we see a stock image, the agency lies in the image itself, the metadata titles and search categories that pre-define the context of the image in use. This can be referred back to the ideas proposed by Guy Debord in 1967’s Society of the Spectacle, where he refers to “a social relation between people that is mediated by images.”2 The agency lies in a staged representation of everyday life, reinterpreting common situations, interactions, and emotions into a photograph deemed for “maximum stock impact”.3 In 2012, The Atlantic even described stock photography as “becoming its own specialized skill”, in light of the growing demand and needs of stock image users, leading photographers to shoot images optimized for being easily usable and editable.

2 Guy Debord, The Society of the Spectacle, Thesis 4.
3 Megan Garber, The Tao of Shutterstock: What Makes a Stock Photo a Stock Photo?, The Atlantic, 2012.

This exhibition asks if the stock image fits the idea of a “poor image”, a term coined by Hito Steyerl. On its own, the stock image is a highly considered image that is undoubtedly created to fit very specific criteria, as to establish its commercial appeal. However, as the stock image moves through the mass media landscape, whether it’s through webpages in the case of the changing titles behind each image, or the newsrack through printed circulation, the image will begin to degrade in resolution and meaning.4 Hence, as circulators and consumers of stock photography, it is important to understand that the choice of a stock image should not merely be the choice of a picture, but a consideration in meaning.

4 Hito Steyerl, In Defense of the Poor Image, e-flux journal, 2009.
